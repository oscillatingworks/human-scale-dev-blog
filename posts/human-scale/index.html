Why the game is called Human Scale

The game is named after an idea from city planning. “Human scale” means building cities for a person on foot, not for a car. This post explains the idea, and how it shapes every city you will walk through in the game.

A city at 5 km/h

The Danish architect Jan Gehl spent his life watching how people use streets. His main lesson is simple. We walk at about 5 km/h, and our senses are made for that speed. At walking pace we see faces, read shop windows, hear a conversation and notice a door that is open. Many modern streets are designed for cars at 50 or 60 km/h. At that speed, details blur, so the buildings get big and plain, and the street becomes a place you pass through, not a place you are in.

A few rules of thumb come out of his work, and we use them:

  • Details at eye level. You can read a face at about 25 m. What matters most is the ground floor: many narrow shop fronts, doors close together, windows you can look into. A good shop street has 10 to 15 shops in every 100 m. A long blank wall makes people walk faster.
  • Soft edges. Café tables, benches, plants and steps where a building meets the street. People stop and stay where the edge is soft.
  • Streets like outdoor rooms. A street feels comfortable when the buildings are about as tall as the street is wide. Most homes are three to five floors high. Above that, people lose touch with the street below.
  • Everything close. A baker, a pharmacy, a café, a bus stop and a park within about 400 m of home: a five-minute walk.
  • Places to stop. Squares, shade, a fountain, a bench with a view. A city is as much about staying as about going.

The architect David Sim adds an idea we like a lot: a “soft city” is dense, but also varied and close, with layers between home and street (a front door, a shared stair, a courtyard) and small, cheap changes that make daily life easier.

Target maps first

Before our city generator builds a city, our city planner draws a target: a fictional city of that style, with its districts, main streets, blocks, squares, water and bus lines. Each target takes its numbers from real planning practice. Then we tune the generator until its cities match the target, and we measure every city it makes.

The three styles so far:

  • A Mediterranean port town. A tight old town of lanes on a low hill above the harbour, a main square with the town hall, a tree-lined ring road where the old wall stood, a grid of big blocks with cut corners and green courtyards, a rambla (a boulevard with a walk down the middle) to the station, a beach promenade, and fishermen’s low houses by the port.
  • A Central European canal city. An old town inside a ring where the walls were, a market square, a station square, courtyard blocks from around 1900, a garden city from the 1920s, an estate from the 1970s, allotment gardens and a canal.
  • A North American river city. A grid by the river, brownstones and walk-ups, an avenue under an elevated railway, a small downtown with towers, and an old rail yard with lofts.

The cities are small on purpose. One city is about 1.2 by 0.8 km, so you can cross it on foot in 13 to 16 minutes. In the cities we test today, almost every home has its daily needs within 400 m.

Neighbourhoods with their own character

Our first cities had a problem: outside the old town, the homes were one pattern repeated everywhere. It worked, but it was dull to walk through. So every area with homes now splits into neighbourhoods, each with its own street pattern and block size: long rows of narrow houses, a workers’ quarter, a quarter of courtyard blocks from around 1900, a garden quarter. Our target also has a 1960s estate of slabs and towers in open green land. The slabs and towers themselves are coming next. Neighbouring areas are planned to be of a different kind, so a short walk crosses several small worlds. The generator does not always manage that yet, and it gets closer each week.

What this means from above

The game is seen from straight above, at a scale where one metre is a few pixels. From there you cannot see how tall a building is. So human scale has to live in what you can see from above: narrow plots, many doors, shop fronts, steps, planters, café terraces and benches. That is where our art and our city rules meet, and it is what we work on next: lower homes, softer edges where buildings meet the street, and more places to stop.

We will write more about each city as it gets closer to what you will walk through in the game.
